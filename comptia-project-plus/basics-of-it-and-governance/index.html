Sample Questions

What does ESG stand for and why is it increasingly relevant to project management?
  • A. ESG stands for Environmental, Social, and Governance, a framework that evaluates organizations (and projects) across three dimensions: environmental impact (carbon footprint, resource use), social impact (labor practices, diversity, community), and governance (ethics, transparency, compliance); projects increasingly must consider ESG factors because stakeholders, regulators, and investors expect organizations to operate responsibly beyond just financial returns
  • B. ESG stands for Enterprise Software Guidelines, technical standards for software development
  • C. ESG stands for Executive Steering Group, the committee that approves project charters
  • D. ESG is only relevant to publicly traded companies, private organizations and their projects are exempt

ESG FRAMEWORK IN PROJECT MANAGEMENT: ENVIRONMENTAL (E): how a project affects the natural environment; includes: carbon emissions from project activities; energy consumption; waste generated; resource depletion; ecological impact; GREEN IT considerations (data center energy, e-waste); SOCIAL (S): how a project affects people, employees, communities, and society; includes: diversity and inclusion in the project team; labor practices (fair wages, safe working conditions); community impact of the project deliverable; supply chain labor practices (ethical sourcing); employee well-being and work-life balance; GOVERNANCE (G): how a project is directed and controlled ethically; includes: transparency and honest reporting; regulatory compliance; anti-corruption and anti-bribery; data privacy and security; ethical decision-making; board-level oversight; WHY RELEVANT TO PMs: organizations now face ESG reporting requirements (EU Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive, SEC climate disclosure rules); investors use ESG scores to make investment decisions; customers prefer organizations with strong ESG performance; regulatory penalties for ESG violations are increasing; project managers must account for ESG factors in project planning, risk assessment, and execution.

A project manager is planning a large-scale data center upgrade. What ENVIRONMENTAL considerations should be part of the project planning?
  • A. Environmental project considerations include: energy efficiency of new server hardware (Power Usage Effectiveness, PUE); renewable energy sourcing for the data center; heat waste and cooling system efficiency; e-waste disposal plan for decommissioned hardware; carbon offset options for unavoidable emissions; compliance with environmental regulations; embodied carbon in new hardware procurement
  • B. Environmental considerations are not a project manager's responsibility, only the facilities team handles environmental issues
  • C. Environmental factors only apply to construction projects, IT projects have no environmental footprint
  • D. Environmental considerations only affect the final product, not the project activities themselves

ENVIRONMENTAL CONSIDERATIONS for data center upgrade: ENERGY EFFICIENCY: Power Usage Effectiveness (PUE) = total data center energy / IT equipment energy; industry average PUE ≈ 1.57; hyperscaler efficiency ≈ 1.1-1.2; specify PUE targets for new hardware in the requirements; servers: choose Energy Star certified hardware; RENEWABLE ENERGY: can the data center be powered by renewable energy (solar, wind)? are Power Purchase Agreements (PPAs) available? HEAT AND COOLING: hot aisle/cold aisle containment reduces cooling waste; free cooling (using outside air or water when temperature allows) reduces energy; waste heat recovery (using server heat for building heating). E-WASTE: where do decommissioned servers go?; responsible e-waste recycling certification (R2, e-Stewards); avoid sending e-waste to developing countries; CARBON FOOTPRINT: calculate the project's Scope 1 (direct), Scope 2 (electricity), and Scope 3 (supply chain) emissions; identify carbon offset opportunities; HARDWARE PROCUREMENT: ask vendors about their manufacturing emissions; consider refurbished or remanufactured equipment to reduce embodied carbon; REGULATORY COMPLIANCE: applicable environmental regulations for IT disposal and energy use.

A project manager is assembling a team for a 9-month healthcare app development project. She wants to apply diversity and inclusion (D&I) principles to team formation and project execution. What specific practices should she implement?
  • A. D&I practices in project team formation and execution: consider diverse candidate pools when requesting team members from functional managers; set inclusive meeting norms (language, time zones, and accessibility); ensure psychological safety (all team members' ideas are heard and valued); provide equitable opportunities (all team members get meaningful assignments, not just senior ones); be aware of unconscious bias in task assignment (ensure junior or underrepresented team members aren't always assigned support roles)
  • B. D&I only applies to corporate HR policies, individual project teams are assembled based purely on technical skills
  • C. D&I means ensuring all team members have identical assignments regardless of skill, equal work distribution
  • D. D&I only applies to gender diversity, other dimensions of diversity are not relevant to project teams

D&I PRACTICES in project team formation and execution: TEAM FORMATION: request team members from diverse talent pools when working with functional managers; if all available candidates are from the same demographic, flag this as a concern; avoid defaulting to 'people I know' (which perpetuates existing homogeneity); consider cognitive diversity, different thinking styles and backgrounds approach problem-solving differently (researchers consistently find that diverse teams produce better solutions for complex problems like healthcare app design); INCLUSIVE MEETING PRACTICES: time zone equity, don't always schedule meetings at times that are inconvenient for one office location; language accessibility, avoid jargon that creates barriers for non-native speakers; active inclusion, solicit input from quieter team members, not just the most vocal; PSYCHOLOGICAL SAFETY: team members should feel safe raising quality concerns, dissenting opinions, or admitting mistakes without fear of retaliation; the PM models this by acknowledging their own mistakes and encouraging candid feedback; EQUITABLE OPPORTUNITY: meaningful project roles and leadership opportunities should be distributed across team members, not concentrated among the most senior; ACCESSIBLE DELIVERABLES: the healthcare app should be designed with accessibility in mind from the start (WCAG compliance for users with visual, motor, or cognitive disabilities); UNCONSCIOUS BIAS AWARENESS: be conscious of patterns in task assignment; if all the visible/presentable work goes to some team members and all the hidden/support work goes to others, that's a bias pattern worth examining.

A project manager is implementing a new HR system for a company with employees in the EU and US. During planning, she identifies multiple regulatory frameworks that affect the project. What GOVERNANCE approach should she take to ensure regulatory compliance is built into the project?
  • A. Integrate compliance requirements as project requirements from the start: identify all applicable regulations (GDPR for EU employees, CCPA for California employees, HIPAA if health data is involved, SOX if financial data is included); engage the legal and compliance team as project stakeholders; add compliance requirements to the requirements document; include compliance testing in the QA plan; build privacy-by-design into the architecture; schedule regulatory review as a project milestone
  • B. Compliance is the legal team's responsibility, the PM delivers the system and legal handles compliance after
  • C. Compliance requirements are added at go-live during the final testing phase, earlier is too speculative
  • D. Only the regulations the PM personally knows about need to be addressed, it's not the PM's job to research regulations

REGULATORY COMPLIANCE INTEGRATION in project planning: IDENTIFY ALL APPLICABLE REGULATIONS: GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation): applies to processing personal data of EU residents; requires: consent management; right to erasure; data minimization; privacy notices; data breach notification; DPA (Data Processing Agreement) with any third-party vendors; CCPA (California Consumer Privacy Act): similar to GDPR for California residents; HIPAA: if the HR system captures health-related data (disability accommodations, medical leave); SOX (Sarbanes-Oxley): if HR data feeds financial controls (payroll, equity compensation); ENGAGE COMPLIANCE AS STAKEHOLDERS: add Data Protection Officer (DPO) or legal counsel to the stakeholder register; compliance requirements must come from people who understand the regulations; REQUIREMENTS INTEGRATION: GDPR-specific requirements: 'Users can request deletion of their personal data within 30 days'; 'Personal data is not retained beyond 7 years'; 'Data is encrypted in transit and at rest'; these go into the requirements document, not in a separate compliance document; PRIVACY-BY-DESIGN in architecture: data minimization (collect only what's needed); purpose limitation (use data only for its stated purpose); storage limitation (automated deletion after retention period); COMPLIANCE TESTING: specific test cases for each regulatory requirement; REGULATORY REVIEW MILESTONE: a phase gate where the legal/compliance team formally reviews and approves the design before development begins.

A project is in a critical 3-month delivery phase. The PM notices the team is working consistently 60-hour weeks. A key developer says they're exhausted. The PM is tempted to continue the pace to 'push through' to delivery. What SOCIAL ESG considerations should inform the PM's decision?
  • A. The PM should maintain the 60-hour weeks, delivery commitments override employee wellbeing
  • B. The developer's complaint should be ignored, professional project team members accept whatever hours are required
  • C. Social ESG considerations support reducing the unsustainable pace: employee wellbeing is a social responsibility dimension; sustained overwork produces diminishing quality returns, increases defect rates, and increases burnout/attrition risk; alternative approaches (scope reduction, timeline extension, additional resources) should be explored before accepting team burnout as the solution
  • D. Wellbeing concerns are HR's responsibility, the project manager should not get involved

SOCIAL ESG AND EMPLOYEE WELLBEING: DIRECT SOCIAL RESPONSIBILITY: employee wellbeing is explicitly a Social ESG factor; organizations that systematically overwork employees are failing on the social dimension of ESG; the PM who enables or mandates unsustainable hours is contributing to this failure; PRODUCTIVITY RESEARCH: 'crunch culture' data shows: productivity peaks at approximately 50 hours per week; beyond 55 hours, productivity per hour DECREASES; after 3-4 weeks of 60+ hours, cumulative output of 60 hours = roughly same as 50 hours (diminishing returns); defect rates increase 15-20% in the fourth week of crunch; ALTERNATIVE APPROACHES (the PM should evaluate): SCOPE REDUCTION: can low-priority features be deferred to Phase 2?; TIMELINE EXTENSION: can the delivery date be extended by 2-4 weeks? (often less costly than burnout-induced quality failures); ADDITIONAL RESOURCES: can a contractor be temporarily added to relieve the critical path bottleneck?; SCOPE NEGOTIATION: can the stakeholder accept a reduced MVP at the original date?; PM RESPONSIBILITY: the PM is not just a deliverer of features, they are responsible for the team's sustainable operation; raising this concern with the sponsor ('we can hit the date but the current pace is unsustainable, here are the options') is professional project management.

A project manager discovers that a vendor selection decision was influenced by a personal relationship between the evaluation committee chair and the winning vendor's sales director (the chair received free sports tickets). This is a conflict of interest that was not disclosed. What GOVERNANCE ESG obligations arise and what should the PM do?
  • A. Ignore it, the winning vendor technically met all the evaluation criteria
  • B. Confront the committee chair privately, personal resolution is more appropriate than formal reporting
  • C. This is a conflict of interest and potential corruption issue requiring escalation: the PM should document the concern; report it through the organization's ethics reporting channel (or to the legal/compliance department); the concern must be investigated to determine whether the vendor selection was fair; if the selection was compromised, the procurement may need to be reconsidered, the governance ESG principle of fair, transparent decision-making was violated
  • D. Mention it casually at the next team meeting, informal awareness is sufficient for governance issues

GOVERNANCE ESG, CONFLICT OF INTEREST AND ANTI-CORRUPTION: WHAT HAPPENED: vendor selection committee chair received gifts (sports tickets) from a vendor bidder; the chair did not disclose this relationship; this is a CONFLICT OF INTEREST, the chair had a personal interest that may have influenced their evaluation; depending on jurisdiction and organization policy, this may also constitute BRIBERY (vendor provided value to influence a public official or corporate decision-maker). GOVERNANCE ESG OBLIGATIONS: ANTI-CORRUPTION: ESG governance requires honest, unbiased decision-making; personal gifts influencing procurement decisions corrupt this principle; TRANSPARENCY: the decision-making process should be transparent, undisclosed relationships undermine this; FAIRNESS: all vendors must compete on a level playing field, personal relationships create unfair advantages. PM OBLIGATIONS: PMI Code of Ethics requires PMs to report unethical behavior through appropriate channels; DOCUMENT: record what was observed and when; REPORT: use the organization's ethics hotline, compliance department, or ombudsperson; do NOT report to the committee chair directly (they are the subject); ESCALATION: if internal reporting channels are compromised, external reporting (board audit committee, regulatory authority) may be appropriate; INVESTIGATION: the organization should investigate whether the selection was influenced; potentially re-run the procurement with the chair recused; PROTECTION: whistleblower protection laws protect employees who report in good faith.

An organization is reviewing two competing project proposals for the same budget: Project Alpha (new marketing automation platform, expected ROI 180%); Project Beta (employee well-being digital health platform, expected ROI 95% but significantly improves employee mental health and engagement scores). Using an ESG lens, how should the organization evaluate these projects?
  • A. Select Project Alpha, ROI is always the primary selection criterion and higher ROI wins
  • B. Select Beta, ESG always overrides financial considerations in project selection
  • C. An ESG-informed evaluation considers multiple dimensions: Alpha has higher financial ROI but only serves business efficiency; Beta has lower financial ROI but contributes to Social ESG (employee wellbeing, mental health, engagement) which may reduce turnover costs (replacing an employee costs 50-200% of annual salary), reduce absenteeism, and increase productivity, when these are quantified, Beta's total value may exceed Alpha's
  • D. ESG considerations only apply after a project is selected, they should not influence project selection decisions

ESG-INFORMED PROJECT EVALUATION: ALPHA (Marketing Automation, ROI 180%): FINANCIAL VALUE: strong (180% ROI on direct marketing efficiency gains); ESG DIMENSION: limited, primarily a business efficiency tool; governance benefit from better marketing data quality; TOTAL VALUE: primarily financial; BETA (Employee Well-Being Platform, ROI 95%): FINANCIAL VALUE: 95% direct ROI; SOCIAL ESG VALUE (quantifiable): TURNOVER REDUCTION: if Beta reduces voluntary turnover by 5% (typical effect of well-being programs), and average replacement cost is $50K/employee: 500 employees × 5% turnover reduction × $50K = $1.25M in saved hiring costs annually; ABSENTEEISM REDUCTION: well-being programs typically reduce sick days by 1-2 days per employee annually; PRODUCTIVITY: engagement improvement has measurable productivity correlation; RECRUITMENT: organizations with strong ESG social programs attract talent more easily (important in tight labor markets); ADJUSTED TOTAL VALUE: when these social benefits are monetized, Beta's 'true' ROI may be 150-200%; GOVERNANCE BENEFIT: demonstrates organizational values alignment; SELECTION FRAMEWORK: both financial ROI and ESG value should be evaluated; QUANTIFY ESG VALUE where possible (the approach above); CONSIDER STRATEGIC ALIGNMENT: does the project advance the organization's stated ESG commitments?; CONSIDER ORGANIZATIONAL RISK: ignoring employee wellbeing in a burnout-prone industry is a talent retention risk.

A project manager is procuring hardware for a manufacturing IoT project. The procurement specifications include price and technical specifications. An ESG officer suggests adding ethical sourcing criteria to the vendor evaluation. What ethical sourcing criteria should be added and why are they relevant?
  • A. Ethical sourcing only applies to food and clothing industries, electronics procurement is unaffected
  • B. Price is the only relevant criterion, ethical considerations drive up costs unnecessarily
  • C. Ethical sourcing criteria for electronics procurement: conflict mineral due diligence (Section 1502 of Dodd-Frank requires disclosure of conflict minerals, tin, tantalum, tungsten, gold, from conflict regions); vendor labor practices (no child labor, forced labor, or unsafe working conditions throughout the supply chain); environmental manufacturing practices (RoHS compliance, restriction of hazardous substances; recycling programs); corporate social responsibility certifications; these are relevant because electronics supply chains often involve regions with known labor and conflict issues
  • D. Ethical sourcing is enforced by governments, companies have no independent responsibility

ETHICAL SOURCING CRITERIA for electronics/IoT hardware: CONFLICT MINERALS (3TG. Tin, Tantalum, Tungsten, Gold): Dodd-Frank Section 1502 (US) and EU Conflict Minerals Regulation require disclosure and due diligence; these minerals are found in many electronic components (capacitors, circuit boards); some mining operations in conflict regions fund armed groups; VENDOR CRITERION: ask vendors to provide Conflict Minerals Reporting Template (CMRT); prefer vendors with Responsible Minerals Initiative (RMI) certification; LABOR PRACTICES IN SUPPLY CHAIN: ask vendors about their factory auditing programs; look for Social Accountability International (SA8000) certification or equivalents; no child labor (ILO Conventions); reasonable working hours (not forced excessive overtime); safe working conditions; ENVIRONMENTAL MANUFACTURING: RoHS Compliance: Restriction of Hazardous Substances (restricts lead, mercury, cadmium in electronics); WEEE (Waste Electrical and Electronic Equipment) compliance for disposal; manufacturer take-back programs for end-of-life hardware; SUSTAINABILITY CERTIFICATIONS: EPEAT (Electronic Product Environmental Assessment Tool); Energy Star; ISO 14001 (environmental management system). WHY RELEVANT: organizations face reputational risk if their supply chain is linked to labor abuses or conflict funding; regulatory reporting requirements are expanding; investors score organizations on supply chain ESG performance; doing the right thing is an organizational value.

Key Terms in This Domain

  • PRINCE2: PRojects IN Controlled Environments: process-based PM method
  • Project management plan: Integrated plan governing execution, baselines, and milestones
  • Non-disclosure agreement (NDA): Contract protecting confidential project information
  • Change control log: Record of change requests and approval status
  • Risk response: mitigate: Reduce probability or impact of a threat
  • Risk response: transfer: Shift threat impact to a third party (e.g., insurance)
  • Scope creep: Uncontrolled expansion of scope without change control
  • Change control board (CCB): Body that reviews and approves change requests
  • Project Management Office (PMO): Function standardizing PM practices across projects
  • Agile: Iterative, incremental delivery embracing change
