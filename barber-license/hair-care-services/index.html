Sample Questions

Hair porosity refers to the hair's ability to
  • A. absorb and retain moisture
  • B. stretch under tension without breaking
  • C. resist chemical processing
  • D. reflect light and appear shiny

Porosity is the hair's capacity to absorb and hold moisture; it is determined by the condition of the cuticle layer, the overlapping scale-like cells that form the outer protective layer of the hair shaft; low porosity hair has tightly packed, flat cuticle scales that resist moisture absorption and chemical penetration; normal porosity hair absorbs and retains moisture adequately; high porosity hair has lifted or damaged cuticle scales that absorb moisture quickly but also release it rapidly, making it prone to dryness and chemical overprocessing.

Tinea capitis on a child's scalp is a contraindication for barbershop services because
  • A. the condition makes the child's hair too coarse to cut
  • B. chemical services are not approved for children under 12
  • C. cutting hair causes more fungal spores to be released into the air
  • D. it is a highly contagious fungal infection that can spread to other clients through contaminated implements and surfaces

Tinea capitis is a dermatophyte fungal infection highly contagious through direct and indirect contact; the dermatophyte spores (particularly from Trichophyton and Microsporum species) are shed from infected hair and scalp and can survive on contaminated combs, clippers, caps, and pillow surfaces for extended periods; performing any barbershop service on an infected scalp risks contaminating all implements and surfaces used during that service, potentially transmitting the infection to subsequent clients.

To assess hair elasticity, the barber should
  • A. smell the hair to detect product buildup
  • B. count the number of hairs per square inch of scalp
  • C. gently stretch a single wet strand and observe whether it returns to its original length without breaking
  • D. apply heat to the hair and observe how quickly it dries

Elasticity is assessed by gently stretching a single wet hair strand between two fingers and releasing it; healthy hair with good elasticity will stretch up to 50% of its length when wet and spring back without breaking; hair with poor elasticity (from moisture deficiency or chemical damage) will either break quickly without stretching or stretch but not return to its original length, indicating compromised cortical protein integrity, a contraindication for further chemical services.

A client with active psoriasis plaques on the scalp may receive a basic haircut, but the barber should
  • A. use strong medicated shampoo to treat the plaques during the shampoo service
  • B. use gentle technique, avoid aggressive brushing or scrubbing of plaques, and not perform any chemical services over affected areas
  • C. reschedule all services until the plaques completely resolve
  • D. apply a cortisone cream to the plaques before cutting to reduce inflammation

Psoriasis plaques are not contagious, so basic hair cutting can proceed; however, the Koebner (isomorphic) phenomenon means skin trauma to a psoriatic plaque can trigger new psoriatic lesions at the trauma site; the barber should use gentle technique, avoiding aggressive scrubbing of plaques during shampooing, not performing chemical services (which involve mechanical and chemical scalp trauma) on affected areas, and working around plaques carefully to minimize irritation.

A client with coarse, resistant natural hair requests a permanent wave. The appropriate perm formula is
  • A. an alkaline wave with a higher pH to adequately penetrate the resistant cuticle
  • B. any perm formula since hair type does not affect formula selection
  • C. the mildest available acid wave to protect the coarse hair
  • D. a thio-free (non-thio) wave designed for sensitized or color-treated hair

Coarse, resistant hair (low porosity, tightly packed cuticle) requires a perm formula with sufficient alkalinity to open the cuticle and allow penetration of the reducing agent into the cortex within a practical processing time; alkaline waves (pH 8.2-9.6 using ammonium thioglycolate) provide the cuticle-swelling power needed to penetrate resistant hair; attempting to process resistant coarse hair with a mild acid wave would result in inadequate or uneven curl formation due to insufficient chemical penetration.

During a scalp analysis, a barber observes excessive sebum, visible inflammation, and flaking. These findings suggest
  • A. normal scalp function with temporary seasonal dryness
  • B. scalp conditions such as seborrheic dermatitis that may require treatment adjustment and possible medical referral
  • C. the client has recently used a new shampoo brand
  • D. the scalp is in perfect health and requires no additional attention

The triad of excessive sebum (oiliness), erythema (redness/inflammation), and scaling (flaking) is the classic presentation of seborrheic dermatitis; this condition requires assessment to determine whether the service should proceed (with appropriate product adjustments), should be modified, or should be deferred pending medical evaluation; a barber who identifies this pattern should note it in the client record, adjust the product selection to avoid further irritation (using gentle, fragrance-free products), and refer to a physician if the condition appears severe or infected.

A client requesting a chemical relaxer discloses they used an at-home relaxer kit two weeks ago. The barber's most important pre-service assessment is to
  • A. confirm the client's preferred brand of relaxer for the current service
  • B. apply the new relaxer immediately since two weeks is sufficient recovery time
  • C. assess the type of previous relaxer used, the hair's current elasticity and integrity, and any signs of scalp irritation, to determine if safe reapplication is possible
  • D. decline all future services since a previous at-home relaxer disqualifies the client from professional chemical services

Brand preference is a product selection consideration, not the critical pre-service assessment priority when a client has recently received a chemical service; the priority is assessing the hair's current integrity and the type of previous chemical used.

A barber assesses a client's hair before a chemical wave service and finds severely compromised elasticity. The MOST appropriate action is to
  • A. proceed with the strongest available wave formula to achieve maximum results quickly
  • B. add extra conditioner to the perm solution to protect the hair during processing
  • C. reduce the processing time by half but proceed with the scheduled service
  • D. decline the chemical wave service, recommend a series of conditioning treatments to restore hair integrity, and reschedule after reassessment

Severely compromised hair elasticity indicates that the cortex proteins are already significantly damaged; applying a chemical wave solution (which must break disulfide bonds through reduction) to structurally weakened hair will very likely cause breakage or dissolving of the already-compromised cortex; the professional and ethical response is to decline the chemical service, communicate the assessment findings clearly to the client, recommend a reparative conditioning program, and reschedule for reassessment when the hair has recovered sufficient structural integrity to safely undergo chemical processing.

Key Terms in This Domain

  • Shampooing and Conditioning: Removes sebum, product buildup, and contaminants. Water temperature warm (~100°F). Conditioner closes cuticle and adds moisture or protein based on condition.
  • Chemical Waving: Alkaline/cold wave (ammonium thioglycolate, pH 8.6-9.5, strong curl, room temp), acid wave (glyceryl monothioglycolate, pH 4.5-7, heat activated, gentle curl), non-thio (cysteamine, sensitive scalp).
  • Contraindications: Open lesions, contagious skin/scalp conditions, recent chemical service damage, severe scalp irritation, known allergy. Refer to physician or reschedule.
  • Scalp Massage: Effleurage (light gliding), petrissage (kneading), tapotement (tapping), friction (deep circular). Stimulates circulation; relaxes muscles; supports hair-growth conditions.
  • pH Scale: 0-14: 0-6 acidic, 7 neutral, 8-14 alkaline. Hair and skin are mildly acidic (4.5-5.5). Alkaline products (relaxers, alkaline waves) open the cuticle; acidic ones (rinses, neutralizers) close it.
  • Draping: Chemical drape (water/chemical-resistant) for color and chemical services. Shampoo drape (water-resistant). Cutting cape (cloth/nylon). Neck strip or towel between cape and skin.
  • Haircutting Principles: Sectioning, elevation (0-180°), finger angle, guide (stationary vs traveling), tension. Shape determined by cutting line and elevation. Outlining defines the perimeter; neck shave finishes.
  • Hair Styling: Wet (finger wave, pin curl), blowdry (round/paddle brush), thermal (curling iron, flat iron, marcel), natural styles (braids, locks). Thermal tools require dry hair to prevent damage.
  • Chemical Relaxers: Hydroxide (sodium/lithium/potassium: strong, no pre-shampoo, pH 12+, permanent), thio (ammonium thioglycolate, milder, pH 9-10), keratin (formaldehyde-based smoothing, not a true relaxer).
  • Hair Disorders: Alopecia (areata, androgenetic, traction), trichoptilosis (split ends, only cutting permanently removes), canities (gray), pediculosis capitis (lice, referral, not service).
