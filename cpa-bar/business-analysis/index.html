Sample Questions

An analyst computes an entity's current ratio of 1.8x and quick ratio of 0.9x. Which interpretation of these ratios is MOST accurate?
  • A. The current ratio indicates adequate overall liquidity, but the quick ratio below 1.0x suggests the entity relies heavily on inventory to meet current obligations, raising a concern about near-term liquidity if inventory cannot be converted quickly
  • B. Both ratios indicate the entity is highly liquid, with substantial cushion to meet short-term obligations
  • C. The quick ratio below 1.0x indicates insolvency, the entity cannot pay any of its current liabilities
  • D. The difference between the ratios is immaterial; both indicate the same level of liquidity and no further analysis is warranted

Current ratio = Current Assets / Current Liabilities = 1.8x (satisfactory liquidity at the broad level). Quick ratio = (Cash + Short-term Investments + Net Receivables) / Current Liabilities = 0.9x. The divergence (1.8x vs. 0.9x) signals that inventory comprises a large portion of current assets. If inventory turns slowly, is obsolete, or cannot be liquidated at book value, the entity may struggle to pay current liabilities on time. Industries with fast-moving inventory (grocery, retail) may tolerate this gap; industries with slow-moving inventory (specialty manufacturing, real estate) may face real liquidity stress. The analyst should examine inventory turnover and days inventory outstanding for context.

An entity reports net income of $4,200,000, interest expense of $800,000, income tax expense of $1,200,000, depreciation of $900,000, and amortization of $300,000. Calculate EBITDA.
  • A. $7,400,000, EBITDA adds back interest expense, income tax expense, depreciation, and amortization to net income
  • B. $4,200,000, EBITDA equals net income before any adjustments
  • C. $5,100,000, EBITDA adds back only depreciation and amortization to net income
  • D. $6,600,000, EBITDA adds back income taxes and D&A but not interest expense

EBITDA calculation: Net income: $4,200,000. + Interest expense: $800,000. + Income tax expense: $1,200,000. + Depreciation: $900,000. + Amortization: $300,000. = EBITDA: $7,400,000. EBITDA is widely used as a proxy for operating cash flow and as a valuation metric (EV/EBITDA multiples) because it removes the effects of financing decisions (interest), tax jurisdictions, and non-cash accounting charges (D&A), facilitating cross-company comparison. However, EBITDA has limitations: it excludes working capital changes, capital expenditure requirements, and can overstate cash generation for capital-intensive businesses.

An entity's gross margin decreased from 42% to 34% year-over-year while revenue grew 15%. Which combination of factors MOST likely explains this pattern?
  • A. Revenue growth of 15% always causes gross margin compression because selling more product dilutes the margin percentage
  • B. Rising cost of goods sold (e.g., higher raw material costs, wage inflation in production, or supply chain disruption premiums) that increased faster than the 15% revenue growth, reducing gross margin despite the revenue increase
  • C. Gross margin compression with revenue growth proves that management is misreporting revenue by recording cost of goods sold as operating expense
  • D. Gross margin compression with revenue growth always indicates a product mix shift toward lower-margin products, no other explanation exists

Multiple factors commonly explain gross margin compression alongside revenue growth: (1) Input cost inflation: raw material price increases (e.g., steel, lumber, semiconductors), freight and logistics surcharges, energy cost spikes, if the entity cannot pass these costs through to customers via price increases, gross margin falls; (2) Labor cost increases in manufacturing/production: wage inflation, overtime premiums, reduced productivity; (3) Product mix shift: if the 15% revenue growth came disproportionately from lower-margin products or customer segments (e.g., new large customers negotiating volume discounts), blended gross margin falls; (4) Pricing pressure: competitive pricing required to achieve 15% growth at the expense of margin; (5) Inventory accounting impact: if LIFO is used during rising costs, COGS increases faster. Analytical approach: disaggregate COGS by component and compare growth rates to revenue growth to isolate the primary driver.

A technology company reports adjusted net income of $120 million for the period, which excludes $35 million of stock-based compensation, $18 million of acquisition-related amortization, and $22 million of restructuring charges. GAAP net income is $45 million. A prospective investor is analyzing the company. Which statement BEST reflects the appropriate use of adjusted net income?
  • A. Adjusted net income should be used exclusively, GAAP net income is irrelevant because GAAP accounting does not reflect economic reality for technology companies
  • B. Adjusted net income is useful for comparing recurring operational performance by removing genuinely non-recurring items, but all three exclusions ($35M SBC, $18M amortization, $22M restructuring) should be critically evaluated, SBC is a recurring operating expense for tech companies and its exclusion materially overstates sustainable earnings
  • C. The investor should average GAAP and adjusted net income to arrive at a balanced metric of $82.5 million
  • D. GAAP net income of $45 million should be used exclusively, non-GAAP measures are prohibited by SEC regulations for investor communications

Non-GAAP measure evaluation framework: (1) Acquisition-related amortization ($18M): often considered legitimate to exclude because amortization of customer relationships and technology acquired in business combinations is a non-cash, accounting-driven charge that does not recur in the absence of acquisitions, many analysts exclude this; (2) Restructuring charges ($22M): judgment required, truly one-time restructuring events may be appropriately excluded; however, if the company restructures every 2-3 years, this is effectively a recurring cost of doing business and should be included in run-rate analysis; (3) Stock-based compensation ($35M, largest exclusion): this is the most controversial exclusion. SBC is: an ongoing compensation expense for attracting and retaining talent; a real economic cost to existing shareholders (dilution); a recurring expense for tech companies that rely on equity compensation. Excluding SBC systematically overstates the economics of the business. The SEC has consistently cautioned against non-GAAP metrics that exclude recurring expenses. An investor who uses the $120M adjusted figure as a basis for valuation may significantly overpay.

A manufacturer's utility cost for the past six months shows the following data: Month 1, 8,000 machine hours, $42,000 cost; Month 6, 14,000 machine hours, $63,000 cost. Using the high-low method, calculate the variable cost per machine hour and total fixed cost.
  • A. Variable cost: $2.50/hr; Fixed cost: $22,000
  • B. Variable cost: $3.50/hr; Fixed cost: $14,000
  • C. Variable cost: $3.50/hr; Fixed cost: $0, the cost is entirely variable
  • D. Variable cost: $4.50/hr; Fixed cost: $6,000

High-low method calculation: (1) Identify high and low activity levels: High = Month 6: 14,000 hours, $63,000; Low = Month 1: 8,000 hours, $42,000; (2) Variable cost per unit = Change in cost / Change in activity = ($63,000 − $42,000) / (14,000 − 8,000) = $21,000 / 6,000 = $3.50 per machine hour; (3) Fixed cost = Total cost − Variable cost × Activity level (use either the high or low data point): Using High: $63,000 − ($3.50 × 14,000) = $63,000 − $49,000 = $14,000. Using Low: $42,000 − ($3.50 × 8,000) = $42,000 − $28,000 = $14,000. Confirm: Both data points yield $14,000 fixed cost. Cost equation: Total Utility Cost = $14,000 + $3.50 per machine hour. The high-low method uses only two data points (extreme high and low) to separate fixed and variable cost components, it is simple but potentially less accurate than regression analysis if the high/low points are outliers.

An entity's actual sales for the period were $4,800,000 against a budget of $5,500,000. The entity sold 48,000 units at an average price of $100, versus a budget of 55,000 units at $100 per unit. Management asks the analyst to quantify the sales volume variance and sales price variance.
  • A. Total variance: $700,000 unfavorable; Volume variance: $480,000 unfavorable; Price variance: $220,000 unfavorable
  • B. Total variance: $700,000 unfavorable; Volume variance: $550,000 unfavorable; Price variance: $150,000 unfavorable
  • C. Total variance: $700,000 unfavorable; Volume variance: $700,000 unfavorable (7,000 units × $100); Price variance: $0, because actual and budgeted price were both $100
  • D. Volume variance: $700,000 favorable because selling 48,000 units instead of 55,000 is efficient; Price variance: $0

Sales variance decomposition: Total variance = Actual sales − Budget = $4,800,000 − $5,500,000 = −$700,000 unfavorable. Volume variance = (Actual units − Budget units) × Budget price = (48,000 − 55,000) × $100 = −7,000 × $100 = −$700,000 unfavorable. Price variance = (Actual price − Budget price) × Actual units = ($100 − $100) × 48,000 = $0. Since actual price equals budgeted price ($100 = $100), the entire $700,000 variance is attributable to volume, the entity sold 7,000 fewer units than budgeted. There is no price variance. Management should investigate why unit volume fell short: market demand shortfall? Competitive pressure? Sales force underperformance? Distribution disruption? Lost key customers?

A hospital system uses a balanced scorecard to measure performance. Under the 'Customer/Patient' perspective, which metric is MOST appropriate?
  • A. Net operating margin, the financial performance of the hospital system's operations after all expenses
  • B. Number of new electronic health record system features deployed, measuring IT development productivity
  • C. Patient satisfaction scores (e.g., HCAHPS survey ratings) and patient readmission rates within 30 days
  • D. Employee turnover rate among nursing staff, measuring the hospital's internal human capital management

The balanced scorecard's four perspectives for a hospital: (1) Financial: operating margin, revenue per adjusted discharge, cost per patient day; (2) Customer/Patient: patient satisfaction (HCAHPS scores), readmission rates, wait times, patient outcomes, net promoter score, patient retention; (3) Internal Processes: length of stay, surgical complication rates, hand hygiene compliance, EHR implementation; (4) Learning and Growth: staff training hours, employee satisfaction, nursing turnover, research publications. Patient satisfaction scores directly measure how patients experience the hospital, the core of the customer perspective. Readmission rates within 30 days measure the quality of care delivered (directly patient-outcome focused) and are tracked by CMS as a quality indicator affecting reimbursement.

A data analytics team uses correlation analysis on an entity's monthly sales data and identifies a 0.92 correlation between marketing spend and revenue. The team also identifies three data points where revenue is significantly higher than the trend predicted. Management wants to use this analysis to conclude that doubling the marketing budget will double revenue. An IS auditor evaluating this analysis identifies the key limitation. Which argument MOST effectively challenges management's conclusion?
  • A. Correlation analysis is not a recognized analytical technique, only regression analysis may be used to draw conclusions about relationships between variables
  • B. A 0.92 correlation is too low to support any conclusion, correlation conclusions require a perfect 1.0 coefficient
  • C. The analysis is valid and management's conclusion is fully supported, a 0.92 correlation directly proves that marketing drives revenue and doubling spend will double output
  • D. Correlation establishes statistical association between variables but does not establish causation, other factors (seasonality, economic conditions, competitor actions, new product launches) may drive both marketing spend and revenue simultaneously; the three outlier data points may indicate the relationship is non-linear or context-dependent, and doubling spend could encounter diminishing returns, audience saturation, or media cost inflation that breaks the historical relationship

Limitations of correlation-to-causation reasoning: (1) Spurious correlation: marketing spend and revenue may both be driven by a third variable, economic growth, for example, could cause both marketing budgets to increase (more available cash) and revenue to rise (more consumer spending); (2) Reverse causation: revenue growth may cause marketing spend increases (companies invest more when performing well), not the other way around; (3) Outlier analysis: the three anomalous data points warrant investigation, they may represent a different market condition under which the 0.92 relationship breaks down, or they may represent data errors; (4) Non-linearity: the 0.92 linear correlation may not hold at doubled spend levels, advertising faces diminishing marginal returns (audience saturation, frequency fatigue, media cost inflation as demand for ad inventory rises); (5) Inference limitation: doubling spend to double revenue would require the relationship to be both causal AND linear AND remain stable out of sample, all three assumptions are unverified.

Key Terms in This Domain

  • Financial Statement Analysis: Current period vs. prior period or budget comparisons; interpretation of fluctuations and ratios (profitability, liquidity, solvency, performance).
  • Capital Structure: Mix of debt and equity financing; cost of capital (WACC); impact on liquidity, leverage, and loan covenants.
  • Investment Valuation: Payback period, NPV, IRR, economic value added, cash flow analysis; comparison of investment alternatives.
  • Business Combinations (ASC 805): Acquisition method; consideration transferred, identifiable net assets, goodwill or bargain purchase gain; measurement period adjustments.
  • Non-Financial and Non-GAAP Measures: Customer retention rate, employee turnover, ticket response time; EBITDA, free cash flow, core earnings, adjusted net income.
  • Variance Analysis: Price/rate, quantity/efficiency, and volume variances for direct materials, direct labor, and overhead.
  • Budgeting and Forecasting: Master budget, flexible budgets, rolling forecasts; sensitivity analysis, what-if scenarios, breakeven analysis, and predictive analytics.
  • Working Capital Management: Strategies for managing receivables, inventory, payables, and short-term financing to balance liquidity and profitability.
  • SWOT Analysis: Strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, threats: strategic framework for assessing business options.
  • Balanced Scorecard: Performance measurement framework spanning financial, customer, internal process, and learning-and-growth perspectives.
