FAA Part 107 is the Remote Pilot Certificate with a small Unmanned Aircraft Systems (sUAS) rating, required by 14 CFR Part 107 for any commercial drone operation in the United States. The initial knowledge test (UAG) is administered by PSI at FAA-approved testing centers and consists of 60 multiple-choice questions covering five Areas of Operation from the FAA Airman Certification Standards (FAA-S-ACS-10B): Regulations, Airspace and Operating Requirements, Weather, Loading and Performance, and Operations. Candidates pass with 70% (42 of 60). After August 2024, recurrent currency is satisfied through the free online ALC-677 training every 24 months instead of a recurrent knowledge test.

The certification validates that you can apply real working knowledge — not just recall facts — across 9 distinct exam domains, with the largest weight on Airspace Classification and Operational Requirements. For candidates competing in the FAA ecosystem, Part 107 is a clear signal to a hiring manager that you have invested in measurable, third-party-verified competence.

Part 107 is most useful for professionals working in or moving toward the FAA ecosystem. In short: The 14 CFR Part 107 Remote Pilot Certificate for commercial drone operations. If you are early in your career, Part 107 is one of the fastest credentials to add to a résumé that recruiters actively screen for. If you are mid-career, it formalizes the skills you already use day-to-day and unlocks roles that gate on it. Exam structure and difficulty

The FAA Part 107 / 14 CFR Part 107 exam is organized into 9 domains, each weighted by the official FAA exam guide. The heaviest weighting is Airspace Classification and Operational Requirements at 20% of the exam — that is where you should spend the most preparation time. The lightest is Remote Identification at 3%, meaning you can dedicate roughly proportional review time without over-investing.

Common pitfalls

The three traps that kill Part 107 candidates: (1) over-memorizing acronyms instead of practicing the application of concepts in scenarios — the exam is scenario-driven, not a vocab quiz. (2) Skipping the heaviest-weighted domain because it feels less interesting — you can fail the whole exam by neglecting Airspace Classification and Operational Requirements. (3) Not timing practice sessions — the exam has a real clock, and pacing is its own skill. Build timing into your last two weeks of prep.
