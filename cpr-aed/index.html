CPR / AED Certification is the lay-rescuer credential for responding to sudden cardiac arrest, choking, and other breathing emergencies. The knowledge component is standardized across the American Heart Association and American Red Cross and is built on the AHA Guidelines for CPR & ECC: scene safety, the Chain of Survival, recognition of cardiac arrest, high-quality compressions and ventilations for adults, children, and infants, AED operation, choking response, and special situations such as opioid overdose, drowning, and pregnancy. Required for many healthcare, education, fitness, lifeguard, and child-care roles, and broadly recommended for any responsible adult.

Hands-only and full-CPR pathways · AED skill check required · 2-year certification cycle (AHA / ARC)

The CPR/AED Guide

Everything you need to know before sitting for CPR / AED Certification

Why CPR/AED matters in 2026

CPR / AED Certification is one of the most recognized credentials issued by American Red Cross / AHA, and 2026 hiring data continues to show CPR/AED on job postings as either required or strongly preferred for the roles it targets. The certification validates that you can apply real working knowledge — not just recall facts — across 7 distinct exam domains, with the largest weight on Adult CPR. For candidates competing in the American Red Cross / AHA ecosystem, CPR/AED is a clear signal to a hiring manager that you have invested in measurable, third-party-verified competence.

Who should take CPR/AED

CPR/AED is most useful for professionals working in or moving toward the American Red Cross / AHA ecosystem. In short: Lifesaving cardiac and breathing emergency skills. If you are early in your career, CPR/AED is one of the fastest credentials to add to a résumé that recruiters actively screen for. If you are mid-career, it formalizes the skills you already use day-to-day and unlocks roles that gate on it. If you are switching tracks, it gives you a structured curriculum — exactly what to study, in what order, weighted by what the real exam tests.

Exam structure and difficulty

The CPR / AED Certification exam is organized into 7 domains, each weighted by the official American Red Cross / AHA exam guide. The heaviest weighting is Adult CPR at 22% of the exam — that is where you should spend the most preparation time. The lightest is Introduction and Chain of Survival at 8%, meaning you can dedicate roughly proportional review time without over-investing.

Format details: Hands-only and full-CPR pathways · AED skill check required · 2-year certification cycle (AHA / ARC). Following the domain weights is the single biggest leverage point candidates miss — many over-study lower-weighted material because it feels comfortable.

How to prepare

A practical four-step plan for CPR/AED:

  1. Read the official American Red Cross / AHA exam guide for CPR/AED and write down every sub-objective. This becomes your study checklist.
  2. Use the domain practice quizzes on this page in weight order (heaviest first). Aim for 80% on each domain before moving on.
  3. When you miss a question, read the explanation for every wrong answer — that contrastive learning is where understanding compounds.
  4. Once every domain is at 80%+, take the Mix Quiz repeatedly to simulate real exam conditions across all topics.

Career outcomes and salary

Holders of CPR/AED in the US currently see compensation in the range of $35k–$65k per year, with median around $48k. Required across many roles; not a standalone job Salary varies by region, employer size, and complementary skills, but the CPR/AED credential consistently lifts the floor of what you can negotiate against.

Common pitfalls

The three traps that kill CPR/AED candidates: (1) over-memorizing acronyms instead of practicing the application of concepts in scenarios — the exam is scenario-driven, not a vocab quiz. (2) Skipping the heaviest-weighted domain because it feels less interesting — you can fail the whole exam by neglecting Adult CPR. (3) Not timing practice sessions — the exam has a real clock, and pacing is its own skill. Build timing into your last two weeks of prep.
