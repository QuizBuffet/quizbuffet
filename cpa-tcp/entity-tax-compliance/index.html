Sample Questions

Under current federal tax law applicable to C corporations, what are the carryover rules for a net operating loss (NOL) generated in a tax year beginning after December 31, 2017?
  • A. NOLs may only be carried forward indefinitely, with the deduction in any carryover year limited to 80% of the corporation's taxable income (computed before the NOL deduction) for that year
  • B. NOLs may be carried back two years and forward twenty years, and there is no limitation on the amount that can be used in any carryover year
  • C. NOLs may be carried back two years and forward twenty years, but the deduction in any carryover year is limited to 80% of taxable income
  • D. NOLs generated by C corporations expire after five years and cannot be carried back under any circumstances

Under TCJA §13302, for NOLs arising in taxable years beginning after December 31, 2017: (1) Carryback: generally eliminated for C corporations (exceptions remain for farming losses, which retain a 2-year carryback); (2) Carryforward: indefinite, NOLs do not expire; (3) Annual deduction limitation: 80% of the corporation's taxable income (computed without regard to the NOL deduction) for each carryover year. These changes replaced the prior-law rules that allowed 2-year carryback and 20-year carryforward with no percentage limitation on the annual deduction amount.

Under IRC §351, a taxpayer contributes property with a fair market value of $90,000 and an adjusted basis of $30,000 to a newly formed corporation in exchange for 100% of the stock. No other consideration is received. What is the tax treatment for the contributing taxpayer?
  • A. The taxpayer recognizes no gain or loss; the taxpayer's basis in the stock received equals $30,000 (substituted basis equal to the adjusted basis of the property contributed)
  • B. The taxpayer recognizes a $60,000 taxable gain equal to the difference between FMV and basis, because transfers to corporations always trigger gain recognition
  • C. The taxpayer recognizes no gain or loss; the taxpayer's basis in the stock received equals $90,000 (the fair market value of the stock received)
  • D. The taxpayer recognizes no gain or loss only if the contributed property has been held for more than one year, short-term property always triggers gain recognition on §351 exchanges

Under §351(a), no gain or loss is recognized when property is transferred to a corporation solely in exchange for stock if the transferors are in control (≥80%) immediately after the exchange. The taxpayer's basis in the stock received equals the adjusted basis of the property contributed ($30,000) under §358, a substituted (carryover) basis. The $60,000 of built-in gain is deferred, not eliminated; it will be recognized when the stock is later sold. The corporation's basis in the received property is also a carryover basis of $30,000 under §362.

XYZ Corporation has a $500,000 NOL carryforward from a prior year (post-2017). In the current year, XYZ has taxable income of $300,000 before applying the NOL deduction. What is XYZ's taxable income after applying the NOL deduction, and what amount of NOL carries forward to future years?
  • A. Taxable income = $0; NOL carryforward = $200,000, the full NOL offsets income until exhausted
  • B. Taxable income = $60,000; NOL carryforward = $260,000, the 80% limitation allows deduction of $240,000 ($300,000 × 80%), leaving $60,000 of taxable income and $260,000 of NOL remaining
  • C. Taxable income = $0; NOL carryforward = $500,000, post-2017 NOLs cannot reduce taxable income below zero
  • D. Taxable income = $150,000; NOL carryforward = $350,000, only 50% of the NOL may be used in any single year

Post-2017 NOL deduction calculation: (1) Taxable income before NOL = $300,000; (2) 80% limitation = $300,000 × 80% = $240,000 maximum NOL deduction; (3) Available NOL = $500,000 > $240,000 limitation, so deduction = $240,000; (4) Taxable income after NOL = $300,000 − $240,000 = $60,000; (5) NOL carryforward to future years = $500,000 − $240,000 = $260,000 (indefinite carryforward). The 80% limitation intentionally ensures that corporations with NOL carryforwards still pay some corporate tax in profitable years, at least on 20% of their current income.

GreenCo Corporation distributes property (land) with a fair market value of $120,000 and an adjusted basis of $45,000 to its sole shareholder as a nonliquidating distribution. GreenCo has current and accumulated E&P of $200,000. What are the tax consequences to GreenCo and to the shareholder?
  • A. GreenCo recognizes no gain on the distribution; the shareholder recognizes $75,000 of ordinary dividend income
  • B. GreenCo recognizes a $75,000 gain on the distribution under §311(b); E&P is increased by the $75,000 gain and then reduced by the $120,000 FMV of the distributed property; the shareholder recognizes $120,000 of dividend income (FMV of property received)
  • C. GreenCo recognizes a $75,000 gain; the shareholder's basis in the land is $45,000 (GreenCo's carryover basis)
  • D. Neither GreenCo nor the shareholder recognizes income because nonliquidating property distributions are tax-free to both parties

Corporation-level tax consequences under §311(b): (1) GreenCo recognizes $75,000 gain ($120,000 FMV − $45,000 basis) as if it sold the land; (2) E&P adjustment: E&P is first increased by the $75,000 recognized gain, then reduced by the FMV of the distributed property ($120,000); net E&P reduction = $120,000 − $75,000 = $45,000 (essentially the property's adjusted basis). Shareholder tax consequences: (1) Since E&P ($200,000 + $75,000 gain increase = $275,000) exceeds the distribution amount ($120,000), the full $120,000 distribution is a dividend; (2) The shareholder's basis in the land is its FMV at the distribution date ($120,000) under §301(d). Contrast with §311(a): a corporation does not recognize loss when it distributes property that has declined in value (FMV < basis), losses are disallowed on nonliquidating distributions.

ParentCo and its wholly owned subsidiary SubCo file a consolidated return. During the year, ParentCo sells inventory to SubCo for $500,000. The inventory cost ParentCo $350,000 to produce. By year end, SubCo has sold 60% of this inventory to unrelated third-party customers and still holds 40% in ending inventory. How is this intercompany transaction treated in the consolidated return?
  • A. The full $150,000 intercompany profit ($500,000 − $350,000) is recognized in the consolidated return in the year of the original sale from ParentCo to SubCo
  • B. 60% of the intercompany profit ($90,000) is recognized in the consolidated return when SubCo sells to third parties; the remaining 40% ($60,000) is deferred until SubCo sells the remaining inventory to third parties
  • C. No gain or profit is recognized in the consolidated return from the intercompany sale, intercompany transactions are permanently eliminated and no gain ever appears
  • D. SubCo includes the full $500,000 purchase price as cost of goods sold regardless of the intercompany relationship, and ParentCo separately reports $150,000 gain on its member return

Consolidated return intercompany transaction rules: (1) The $150,000 intercompany profit is deferred at the time of the ParentCo-to-SubCo sale, no current recognition in the consolidated return; (2) As SubCo sells inventory to third-party customers, the deferred intercompany profit is ratably recognized: 60% sold = $150,000 × 60% = $90,000 recognized; (3) 40% still in ending inventory = $150,000 × 40% = $60,000 remains deferred; (4) SubCo's cost basis in the inventory for consolidated return purposes is the arm's-length intercompany price ($500,000), with the deferred profit reducing this to the $350,000 original cost for tax purposes. This deferral mechanism prevents the group from manipulating the timing of income recognition through intercompany transactions.

ABC Corporation has a $600,000 NOL carryforward attributable to pre-ownership-change periods. During the current year, an ownership change within the meaning of IRC §382 occurred. The fair market value of ABC's stock immediately before the ownership change was $4,000,000. The applicable long-term tax-exempt rate is 3.5%. What is ABC's §382 annual limitation on its pre-change NOL?
  • A. $600,000, the full NOL is available because §382 only limits NOLs if the ownership change was a hostile takeover
  • B. $0, a §382 ownership change permanently disallows all pre-change NOLs
  • C. $140,000, the annual §382 limitation is the FMV of the loss corporation's stock ($4,000,000) multiplied by the long-term tax-exempt rate (3.5%)
  • D. $300,000, exactly 50% of the pre-change NOL, since a 50% ownership change triggers the §382 limitation

The §382 annual limitation = FMV of loss corporation's stock immediately before the ownership change × long-term tax-exempt rate. Calculation: $4,000,000 × 3.5% = $140,000 per year. This $140,000 annual limit restricts how much of the pre-change NOL can be used to offset income in each post-change year. Rationale: §382 approximates the return the acquiring party could have earned investing in tax-exempt bonds, preventing the purchase of loss corporations primarily for their tax benefits. Unused annual limitations accumulate (if income is less than the limitation in a year, the excess carries over to increase the next year's limitation).

A shareholder owns 1,000 shares of RedCo, a C corporation, with an adjusted basis of $40,000. RedCo distributes $60,000 cash to the shareholder. RedCo has no current or accumulated E&P. What are the tax consequences to the shareholder?
  • A. The shareholder recognizes $60,000 of ordinary dividend income because all corporate distributions are taxable as dividends
  • B. The shareholder recognizes $60,000 of long-term capital gain because distributions from C corporations are always capital gain when E&P is zero
  • C. The distribution is $40,000 tax-free return of basis (reducing the shareholder's basis in RedCo to $0), and the remaining $20,000 is treated as gain from the sale or exchange of the stock (capital gain, long-term if the stock is held more than one year)
  • D. The entire $60,000 is tax-free to the shareholder because distributions from corporations without E&P are always nontaxable

Distribution ordering rules under §301(c): (1) Dividend to extent of current and accumulated E&P = $0 (no E&P); (2) Return of capital to extent of the shareholder's adjusted basis: $40,000, the shareholder's basis in the stock is reduced to $0; (3) Treated as gain from the sale or exchange of property for the excess: $60,000 − $40,000 = $20,000 of capital gain (long-term if the stock has been held more than one year). The shareholder's basis in RedCo stock after the distribution = $0 (reduced by the $40,000 return of basis). The capital gain character reflects that the shareholder has received more than their investment in the stock, similar to receiving excess proceeds from selling the stock.

DEF Corporation has the following history: Year 1, $80,000 net capital gain; Year 2, $0 capital gain/loss; Year 3, $150,000 net capital loss; Year 4, $60,000 net capital gain. Apply the C corporation capital loss carryback and carryforward rules to determine DEF's capital gain or loss for tax purposes in Years 3 and 4.
  • A. Year 3: deduct $150,000 capital loss against ordinary income; Year 4: $0 capital loss carryforward remains
  • B. Year 3: no current deduction, $150,000 capital loss carries back to Year 1, absorbing $80,000 of capital gain and leaving $70,000 to carry forward; Year 4: the $70,000 carryforward absorbs the $60,000 of Year 4 capital gain, leaving $10,000 of capital loss to carry forward to Year 5
  • C. Year 3: the $150,000 capital loss creates a $150,000 NOL for the corporation; Year 4: the NOL offsets the $60,000 capital gain, leaving $90,000 NOL carryforward
  • D. Year 3: the $150,000 capital loss carries back to Year 1 ($80,000 absorbed), then forward to Years 4 and beyond for the remaining $70,000; Year 4: $70,000 carryforward absorbs $60,000 of Year 4 gain, resulting in $10,000 capital loss carryforward to Year 5

C corporation capital loss rules (IRC §1212): (1) Capital losses can only offset capital gains, never ordinary income; (2) Net capital losses carry BACK 3 years (to Years with capital gains) and FORWARD 5 years; when carried back, capital losses are treated as short-term regardless of their original character. Year 3 analysis: $150,000 net capital loss in Year 3. Carry back to Year 1 (3 years prior): $80,000 Year 1 capital gain absorbed → $80,000 refund potential; Carry back to Year 2: $0 Year 2 capital gain, nothing absorbed; Remaining carryforward: $150,000 − $80,000 = $70,000 carries forward. Year 4 analysis: $70,000 carryforward from Year 3 + $60,000 Year 4 capital gain → $70,000 − $60,000 = $10,000 net capital loss remaining; this $10,000 carries forward to Years 5-8 (within the 5-year forward window from Year 3, running through Year 8).

Key Terms in This Domain

  • Partnership Partner Basis: Outside basis: contributions of cash and property, share of partnership debt (recourse vs. nonrecourse), share of income/loss.
  • S Corporation Shareholder Basis: Stock basis and debt basis tracking; effect of contributions, distributions, ordinary income/loss, and separately stated items.
  • Charitable Contributions of Noncash Property: AGI percentage limitations (30%, 50%, 60%); long-term capital gain property at FMV vs. ordinary income property at basis.
  • Shareholder Contributions to a C Corporation: IRC §351 nonrecognition for property contributed in exchange for stock; basis carryover.
  • Partnership Elections: §754 election to adjust inside basis of partnership property following a transfer of interest or distribution.
  • C Corporation NOL Carryforward: Post-2017 NOLs limited to 80% of taxable income; ownership-change limitations under IRC §382.
  • Liquidating and Nonliquidating Corporate Distributions: Dividend vs. return of capital vs. capital gain treatment; impact on E&P; redemption rules under §302.
  • S Corporation AAA and AEP: Accumulated Adjustments Account tracks post-S-election undistributed income; ordering rules for distributions.
  • Entity Selection Tax Planning: Comparison of tax outcomes across sole proprietorship, partnership, LLC, S corp, and C corp for a given fact pattern.
  • Related Party Transactions: IRC §267 disallowance of losses; transferred-basis rules for property sold between related parties.
