Three investors are forming a new business and are deciding between organizing as a C corporation, an S corporation, or a partnership. They anticipate losses in the first two years followed by significant profitability. Which characteristic is UNIQUE to S corporations and restricts their use?

Answer: S corporations are limited to 100 shareholders, may only issue one class of stock, and cannot have nonresident alien shareholders or most entity shareholders (with limited exceptions)

S corporation eligibility requirements under §1361: (1) Maximum 100 shareholders (certain family members count as one shareholder); (2) Only one class of stock permitted, voting vs. nonvoting stock of the same economic terms is permitted, but different economic rights (different dividend or liquidation rights) are not; (3) Shareholders must be US citizens or resident aliens, nonresident alien shareholders are prohibited; (4) Shareholders must generally be individuals, certain trusts, or estates, partnerships, C corporations, and most LLCs cannot be shareholders. These restrictions make S corporations unsuitable for complex ownership structures, foreign investors, or businesses expecting rapid growth through multiple equity classes (venture capital often requires preferred stock with different economic rights).

A C corporation expects taxable income of $500,000 in the current year and has $300,000 of NOL carryforwards from prior years (all post-2017). The corporation wants to minimize its current-year federal income tax. What is the maximum NOL deduction the corporation may claim?

Answer: $400,000, the corporation may deduct 80% of the $500,000 taxable income, which is more than the available $300,000 NOL carryforward, so the full $300,000 is deductible

Post-2017 NOL deduction analysis: (1) 80% limitation = $500,000 taxable income × 80% = $400,000 maximum NOL deduction; (2) Available NOL carryforward = $300,000; (3) Actual NOL deduction = min($400,000 limitation, $300,000 available) = $300,000; (4) Taxable income after NOL deduction = $500,000 − $300,000 = $200,000; (5) Federal tax = $200,000 × 21% = $42,000. The full $300,000 NOL is usable because the 80% cap ($400,000) exceeds the available carryforward. No NOL remains for future years. If the NOL had been $600,000, only $400,000 could be used in the current year (80% of $500,000), with $200,000 carried forward to subsequent years.

A sole shareholder is considering contributing appreciated property (basis $40,000, FMV $150,000) to her wholly owned C corporation in exchange for additional stock. She will own 100% of the corporation before and after the contribution. What are the immediate tax consequences of this §351 exchange?

Answer: Neither the shareholder nor the corporation recognizes gain; the shareholder's stock basis increases by $40,000 (her carryover basis in the contributed property); the corporation's basis in the property is $40,000

§351 exchange consequences: (1) Shareholder: no gain recognized (§351(a)); new stock basis = adjusted basis of contributed property = $40,000 (§358 substituted basis); the $110,000 of unrealized gain is preserved in the reduced stock basis; (2) Corporation: no gain recognized upon receipt of property (§1032); basis in the property = contributing shareholder's basis = $40,000 (§362 carryover basis); the corporation inherits the built-in gain, if it later sells the property for $150,000, it will recognize the $110,000 gain. The §351 exchange defers, but does not eliminate, the built-in gain. If the shareholder withdraws the property through liquidation or distribution, double taxation (corporate gain + shareholder dividend/capital gain) may result, an important planning consideration.
