BLS (Basic Life Support) certifies healthcare professionals to deliver high-quality CPR, use an AED, provide ventilation with barrier devices and bag-mask, relieve choking, and work as part of a high-performance resuscitation team for adults, children, and infants. Required for nurses, physicians, EMTs, paramedics, dental and respiratory professionals, and most clinical roles. Built around the AHA Chain of Survival, 2020 BLS Guidelines (with 2023 focused updates), and the AHA BLS Provider course; renewal is typically every two years.

Everything you need to know before sitting for Basic Life Support (BLS)

Why BLS matters in 2026

Basic Life Support (BLS) is one of the most recognized credentials issued by American Heart Association, and 2026 hiring data continues to show BLS on job postings as either required or strongly preferred for the roles it targets. The certification validates that you can apply real working knowledge — not just recall facts — across 8 distinct exam domains, with the largest weight on Adult BLS (1- and 2-Rescuer). For candidates competing in the American Heart Association ecosystem, BLS is a clear signal to a hiring manager that you have invested in measurable, third-party-verified competence.

Who should take BLS

BLS is most useful for professionals working in or moving toward the American Heart Association ecosystem. In short: Required CPR-level credential for healthcare professionals. If you are early in your career, BLS is one of the fastest credentials to add to a résumé that recruiters actively screen for. If you are mid-career, it formalizes the skills you already use day-to-day and unlocks roles that gate on it. If you are switching tracks, it gives you a structured curriculum — exactly what to study, in what order, weighted by what the real exam tests.

Exam structure and difficulty

The Basic Life Support (BLS) exam is organized into 8 domains, each weighted by the official American Heart Association exam guide. The heaviest weighting is Adult BLS (1- and 2-Rescuer) at 22% of the exam — that is where you should spend the most preparation time. The lightest is Course Concepts and Chain of Survival at 6%, meaning you can dedicate roughly proportional review time without over-investing.

Format details: 25-question multiple-choice exam · Pass score 84% (21 of 25) · 2-year certification · Hands-on skills test required for in-person card. Following the domain weights is the single biggest leverage point candidates miss — many over-study lower-weighted material because it feels comfortable.

How to prepare

A practical four-step plan for BLS:

  1. Read the official American Heart Association exam guide for BLS and write down every sub-objective. This becomes your study checklist.
  2. Use the domain practice quizzes on this page in weight order (heaviest first). Aim for 80% on each domain before moving on.
  3. When you miss a question, read the explanation for every wrong answer — that contrastive learning is where understanding compounds.
  4. Once every domain is at 80%+, take the Mix Quiz repeatedly to simulate real exam conditions across all topics.

Career outcomes and salary

Holders of BLS in the US currently see compensation in the range of $40k–$90k per year, with median around $62k. Required for clinical staff; not a role itself Salary varies by region, employer size, and complementary skills, but the BLS credential consistently lifts the floor of what you can negotiate against.

Common pitfalls

The three traps that kill BLS candidates: (1) over-memorizing acronyms instead of practicing the application of concepts in scenarios — the exam is scenario-driven, not a vocab quiz. (2) Skipping the heaviest-weighted domain because it feels less interesting — you can fail the whole exam by neglecting Adult BLS (1- and 2-Rescuer). (3) Not timing practice sessions — the exam has a real clock, and pacing is its own skill. Build timing into your last two weeks of prep.
