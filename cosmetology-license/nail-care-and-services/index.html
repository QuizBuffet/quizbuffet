Sample Questions

During a pre-service nail analysis, a cosmetologist observes that a client's nails are thin, peeling in horizontal layers at the free edge, and break easily. This condition is called:

Answer: Onychoschisis, a condition in which the nail plate splits or peels horizontally in layers, often caused by repeated wetting and drying, excessive use of nail products, or nutritional deficiencies

Onychoschisis (also called lamellar dystrophy) is the horizontal splitting or peeling of the nail plate in layers at the free edge; the nail plate layers separate from each other due to the disruption of the bonds between the nail plate layers — commonly caused by repeated immersion in water followed by drying, use of harsh nail products, acetone overexposure, or nutritional deficiencies; it presents exactly as described with thin, peeling, easily broken nails.

A client presents for a pedicure and the cosmetologist notices a small, round wart-like growth on the bottom of the client's foot (plantar wart). The cosmetologist should:

Answer: Decline to perform any pedicure service, explain that plantar warts are caused by human papillomavirus (HPV) which is highly contagious and can spread through salon equipment and moisture in the foot bath, and refer the client to a physician for treatment

Plantar warts are caused by HPV (human papillomavirus); the virus is transmitted through direct contact with infected tissue or contaminated surfaces and thrives in warm, moist environments such as pedicure basins and foot baths; the service must be declined, and the client should be referred to a physician for appropriate medical wart treatment; this protects other clients and the cosmetologist from HPV exposure through contaminated water, implements, and surfaces.

A client with thin, splitting nails asks the cosmetologist to recommend a nail strengthener for home use. Which type of nail strengthener is most appropriate for thin, brittle, splitting nails?

Answer: A hardener containing high concentrations of formaldehyde to maximally cross-link nail plate proteins for the greatest possible hardness

High-concentration formaldehyde nail hardeners (above 2–5%) cause excessive crosslinking of nail plate keratin proteins, which makes very thin nails paradoxically more brittle rather than stronger — the over-hardened nail becomes rigid and snaps under mechanical stress rather than flexing; formaldehyde hardeners are also associated with skin sensitization and adverse reactions with prolonged use; they are not the recommended first choice for thin, splitting nails.
