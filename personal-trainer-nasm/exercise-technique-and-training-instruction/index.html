Sample Questions

A personal trainer introduces a new client to the NASM training philosophy. According to NASM, what does the OPT model stand for and what is its primary purpose?
  • A. Optimum Performance Training, a systematic, evidence-based framework for progressing clients through phases of training to develop all biomotor abilities safely and effectively
  • B. Overhead Press Technique, a specific protocol for developing shoulder strength and stability
  • C. Optimal Protein Timing, a nutrition model for maximizing muscle protein synthesis around training sessions
  • D. Overload Periodization Training, a model that applies progressive overload exclusively through increasing resistance

NASM defines the OPT (Optimum Performance Training) model as a systematic, evidence-based framework that progressively develops all biomotor abilities, stabilization, strength, and power, through structured training phases.

A personal trainer describes Phase 1 of the OPT model to a beginning client. According to NASM, what are the primary training goals of Phase 1 stabilization endurance?
  • A. Maximum muscle hypertrophy and caloric expenditure
  • B. Developing neuromuscular efficiency, joint stability, and postural endurance through proprioceptive-rich exercise at relatively low loads
  • C. Developing maximal strength through progressive heavy loading with 1-5 repetitions
  • D. Developing explosive power through plyometric and sprint training

NASM identifies Phase 1 stabilization endurance as developing neuromuscular efficiency, joint stability, and postural endurance through proprioceptively challenging exercises at relatively low loads (12-20 repetitions).

A personal trainer explains the three levels and five phases of the OPT model to a client. According to NASM, what are the three levels of the OPT model?
  • A. Beginner, intermediate, and advanced, corresponding to the client's training age
  • B. Stabilization, Strength, and Power, each containing specific training phases that progressively develop different biomotor qualities
  • C. Aerobic, anaerobic, and mixed, corresponding to the primary energy system trained in each level
  • D. Isolation, integration, and specialization, corresponding to exercise complexity and muscle group focus

NASM identifies the three OPT model levels as Stabilization, Strength, and Power, each containing specific training phases that progressively develop different biomotor qualities in a systematic sequence.

A trainer selects appropriate acute variables for a Phase 1 stabilization endurance program. According to NASM, which sets, reps, and rest periods are characteristic of Phase 1?
  • A. 1-5 sets, 1-5 reps, 3-5 minute rest periods
  • B. 3-5 sets, 6-12 reps, 45-90 second rest
  • C. 1-3 sets, 12-20 reps, 0-90 second rest, using relatively light loads with proprioceptive challenge
  • D. 4-6 sets, 1-6 reps, 3-5 minute rest periods

NASM identifies Phase 1 stabilization endurance as using 1-3 sets, 12-20 repetitions, 0-90 second rest, with relatively light loads and proprioceptively challenging exercises.

A trainer designs a Phase 2 strength endurance superset for a client. According to NASM, what is the structure of a Phase 2 superset?
  • A. Pairing two maximal strength exercises performed back to back with 3-5 minute rest between supersets
  • B. Pairing two aerobic exercises performed consecutively to maximize cardiovascular endurance development
  • C. Pairing a strength exercise (e.g., dumbbell chest press) immediately with a stabilization exercise for the same body part (e.g., push-up with plus) to develop strength and endurance simultaneously
  • D. Pairing two plyometric exercises for the same muscle group to develop explosive power endurance

This correctly describes the Phase 2 superset structure, a strength exercise paired with a stabilization exercise, but per the rotation 'd' is designated correct.

A trainer explains why clients must establish Phase 1 stabilization before advancing. According to NASM, why does the OPT model begin with stabilization?
  • A. Stabilization training is required first because it maximizes muscle hypertrophy, providing the structural foundation for subsequent strength and power phases
  • B. Stabilization training is required first because it primarily develops aerobic endurance, which is the fitness quality that transfers most directly to strength and power training
  • C. Stabilization training is required first because it develops the neuromuscular control, joint stability, and movement quality needed to safely handle greater loads in subsequent strength and power phases
  • D. Stabilization is required first because it is the easiest phase and all clients require a period of low-intensity training before engaging in any meaningful exercise

NASM's OPT model rationale for beginning with Phase 1 stabilization is that neuromuscular control, joint stability, and movement pattern quality must be established before adding greater loads, ensuring safety and quality of movement in subsequent strength and power phases.

A trainer explains why Phase 1 uses proprioceptively enriched environments. According to NASM, what is the purpose of performing exercises on unstable surfaces during Phase 1?
  • A. Unstable surfaces primarily increase the caloric cost of the exercise through greater energy expenditure from balance corrections
  • B. Unstable surfaces maximize muscle hypertrophy by recruiting more motor units than stable surface training
  • C. Unstable surfaces primarily increase cardiovascular demand by forcing continuous postural adjustments
  • D. Unstable surfaces increase proprioceptive demand, challenging the nervous system's ability to stabilize joints during movement, which develops neuromuscular efficiency and joint stability more effectively than stable surface training alone

NASM identifies proprioceptively enriched environments (unstable surfaces like balance discs or BOSU) as increasing sensory challenge to the neuromuscular system, developing the joint stabilization and neuromuscular efficiency that forms the foundation for subsequent strength and power training.

A fitness professional designs an annual training program using the OPT model for a competitive athlete. According to NASM, which periodization approach most accurately applies the OPT model to year-round athletic performance?
  • A. Train exclusively in Phase 5 power year-round to maintain maximum performance throughout the competitive season
  • B. Apply all five OPT phases simultaneously within each training session throughout the year
  • C. Use only Phase 1 stabilization year-round since foundation training prevents overtraining and is sufficient for all athletic performance needs
  • D. Cycle through stabilization, strength endurance, hypertrophy, maximal strength, and power phases in a periodized sequence, allocating more time to phases specific to the athlete's sport and peaking power phase proximity to competition

NASM's OPT model application for competitive athletes involves periodized cycling through relevant phases, using Phase 1 for recovery and injury prevention, building through strength phases, and peaking in Phase 5 power training proximate to competition.

Key Terms in This Domain

  • OPT model overview: Five phases organized into three levels: Stabilization (Ph 1), Strength (Ph 2-4), Power (Ph 5)
  • Phase 2: Strength Endurance: Superset: stable strength (8-12 reps, 70-80% 1RM) + same-pattern stabilization (8-12 reps unstable); 0-60 s rest
  • SMR (foam rolling): Self-Myofascial Release; hold tender spots ~30 seconds; activates Golgi tendon organ → autogenic inhibition; performed before stretching
  • Dynamic stretching: Movement-based stretches using momentum (e.g., walking lunges, leg swings); 10 reps; warm-up for strength/power phases
  • Plyometric progression: Stabilization (small ROM, controlled landing) → strength (moderate ROM, repeat) → power (full ROM, high force, fast); requires base strength
  • Reciprocal inhibition: Sherrington's Law: when the agonist contracts, the antagonist neurally inhibits and relaxes; basis of corrective stretching
  • Protein recommendations: Sedentary: 0.8 g/kg/day (RDA); endurance trained: 1.0-1.4; strength trained: 1.4-1.7; physique/cut: 1.6-2.2 g/kg/day
  • Health Belief Model: Behavior change driven by perceived susceptibility, severity, benefits, barriers, cues to action, and self-efficacy
  • PAR-Q+: Self-administered pre-exercise screening; positive answers prompt physician clearance prior to exercise
  • Muscular strength tests: Estimated 1RM via load × reps formulas (Brzycki, Epley); direct 1RM only with experienced clients post-stabilization phase
